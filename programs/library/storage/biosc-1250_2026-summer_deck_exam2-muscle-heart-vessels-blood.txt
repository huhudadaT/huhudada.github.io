Q: What are the three types of muscle tissue?
A: Skeletal, cardiac, and smooth.

Q: Which muscle types are striated?
A: Skeletal and cardiac.

Q: Which muscle type is non-striated?
A: Smooth.

Q: Which muscle type is voluntary?
A: Skeletal.

Q: Which muscle types are involuntary?
A: Cardiac and smooth.

Q: Is skeletal muscle autorhythmic?
A: No.

Q: Is cardiac muscle autorhythmic?
A: Yes.

Q: What does skeletal muscle attach to?
A: Bone.

Q: How many nuclei does a skeletal muscle fiber have?
A: Multiple.

Q: Where is cardiac muscle found?
A: The heart.

Q: Where is smooth muscle found?
A: Hollow organs.

Q: In single-unit smooth muscle, how do the fibers contract?
A: All at the same time.

Q: What are the four functions of muscle?
A: Body movement, stabilizing position, storing/moving substances, and generating heat.

Q: What do postural muscles do while awake?
A: Constantly contract.

Q: What structures act like valves to store contents in the body?
A: Sphincters.

Q: Muscle property: ability to be controlled by neurons?
A: Electrical excitability.

Q: Muscle property: ability to contract and generate tension?
A: Contractility.

Q: Muscle property: ability to stretch without damage?
A: Extensibility.

Q: Muscle property: ability to return to original shape?
A: Elasticity.

Q: Order of skeletal muscle organization from cell to whole muscle?
A: Muscle fiber → fascicle → muscle.

Q: What is a bundle of muscle fibers called?
A: A fascicle.

Q: What connects skeletal muscle to bone?
A: A tendon.

Q: Another name for a muscle fiber?
A: A muscle cell.

Q: Muscle fibers form in the embryo by fusion of what?
A: Myoblasts.

Q: A large cell with many nuclei is called a what?
A: A syncytium.

Q: When is muscle fiber number set?
A: Before birth.

Q: What are the leftover myoblasts with limited repair function?
A: Satellite cells.

Q: Growth of a muscle cell to a larger size is called?
A: Hypertrophy.

Q: Plasma membrane of a muscle fiber?
A: Sarcolemma.

Q: Cytoplasm of a muscle fiber?
A: Sarcoplasm.

Q: What carries the action potential deep into the muscle fiber?
A: Transverse tubules (T-tubules).

Q: What stores Ca2+ inside the muscle fiber?
A: Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).

Q: The enlarged ends of the SR are called?
A: Terminal cisternae.

Q: What makes up a triad?
A: One T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae.

Q: Contractile part of the fiber made of thin and thick filaments?
A: Myofibrils.

Q: What repeating units make up a myofibril?
A: Sarcomeres.

Q: What structure sits in the center of the sarcomere?
A: The M-line.

Q: What passes through the M-line?
A: Thick filaments.

Q: What separates adjacent sarcomeres?
A: Z-discs.

Q: The A-band corresponds to the length of which filament?
A: The thick filaments (their full length).

Q: Which band contains only thin filaments (no overlap)?
A: The I-band.

Q: Which zone contains only thick filaments (no overlap)?
A: The H-zone.

Q: The thin filament contractile protein?
A: Actin.

Q: The thick filament contractile protein?
A: Myosin.

Q: What binding site does actin contain?
A: The myosin-binding site.

Q: What two binding sites does myosin have?
A: An actin-binding site and an ATP-binding site.

Q: Regulatory protein that covers the myosin-binding site on actin?
A: Tropomyosin.

Q: Regulatory protein that pulls tropomyosin off when it binds Ca2+?
A: Troponin.

Q: What ion does troponin bind to trigger contraction?
A: Ca2+.

Q: Structural protein responsible for muscle elasticity?
A: Titin.

Q: Titin spans from what to what?
A: From the Z-disc to the M-line.

Q: Which structural protein is NOT found in the myofilaments?
A: Titin.

Q: Structural protein in the Z-disc that binds actin to titin?
A: α-actinin.

Q: Structural protein in the M-line that binds titin and connects myosin?
A: Myomesin.

Q: Structural protein that links actin to other structures?
A: Dystrophin.

Q: During contraction, what happens to the I-band and H-zone?
A: They shrink and can disappear.

Q: During contraction, does filament length change?
A: No.

Q: During contraction, everything is pulled toward what?
A: The M-line.

Q: What happens to the sarcomere during contraction?
A: It shortens.

Q: Step 1 of the contraction cycle?
A: ATP hydrolysis (myosin head is energized).

Q: In step 1, myosin hydrolyzes ATP into what?
A: ADP.

Q: Step 2 of the contraction cycle?
A: Myosin binds actin, forming a crossbridge.

Q: The bond between the myosin head and actin is called?
A: A crossbridge.

Q: Step 3 of the contraction cycle?
A: The power stroke.

Q: What happens during the power stroke?
A: The crossbridge pivots, pulling the thin filament toward the center.

Q: Step 4 of the contraction cycle?
A: Detachment of myosin from actin.

Q: What must bind for myosin to detach from actin?
A: A new ATP.

Q: What is the neuromuscular junction?
A: The synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fiber.

Q: What neurotransmitter is released at the neuromuscular junction?
A: Acetylcholine.

Q: What triggers acetylcholine release at the axon terminal?
A: Ca2+ entry.

Q: Acetylcholine binding to the postsynaptic receptor causes what?
A: An end plate potential.

Q: What enzyme breaks down acetylcholine?
A: Acetylcholinesterase.

Q: Resting membrane potential of a muscle fiber?
A: -90 mV.

Q: What kind of potential is the end plate potential?
A: A graded potential.

Q: What ion depolarizes the muscle fiber?
A: Na+.

Q: What ion repolarizes the muscle fiber?
A: K+.

Q: Voltage range of a skeletal muscle action potential?
A: -90 to +30 mV.

Q: What does excitation-contraction coupling link?
A: The muscle action potential to muscle contraction.

Q: Where does excitation-contraction coupling occur?
A: At the triad.

Q: The voltage-sensor Ca2+ channel in the T-tubules?
A: The dihydropyridine (DHP / L-type) receptor.

Q: The Ca2+ release channel in the SR terminal cisternae?
A: The ryanodine receptor.

Q: At rest, what blocks the ryanodine release channel?
A: The DHP receptor.

Q: With an action potential, what does the DHP receptor do?
A: Changes shape and frees the release channel.

Q: After release, Ca2+ binds to what protein?
A: Troponin.

Q: Binding of Ca2+ to troponin exposes what?
A: The myosin-binding site on actin.

Q: What restores Ca2+ levels by pumping it back into the SR?
A: The Ca2+ ATPase pump.

Q: What is rigor mortis?
A: Sustained crossbridges after death due to lack of ATP.

Q: Why can't crossbridges detach in rigor mortis?
A: No ATP is being synthesized.

Q: What eventually breaks down the crossbridges in rigor mortis?
A: Lysosomes.

Q: What is the first and fastest ATP source in skeletal muscle?
A: Creatine phosphate.

Q: What enzyme transfers phosphate from creatine phosphate to ADP?
A: Creatine kinase.

Q: What pathway produces ATP when oxygen is low?
A: Anaerobic glycolysis.

Q: In anaerobic glycolysis, glucose becomes pyruvic acid, then what?
A: Lactic acid.

Q: What molecule acts as the oxygen reservoir in muscle?
A: Myoglobin.

Q: What is the slowest ATP-producing pathway?
A: Aerobic respiration.

Q: Three stages of aerobic respiration?
A: Glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain.

Q: What is muscle fatigue?
A: The inability to maintain contraction over time.

Q: Type of fatigue where the CNS sends a protective STOP signal?
A: Central fatigue.

Q: Four causes of peripheral muscle fatigue?
A: Insufficient ATP, Ca2+ depletion, creatine depletion, and lactic acid buildup.

Q: What is oxygen debt?
A: Increased oxygen consumption after exercise to resupply the body.

Q: Where is lactic acid converted back to glycogen?
A: The liver.

Q: What is a motor unit?
A: One motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates.

Q: How many motor neurons innervate a single muscle fiber?
A: One.

Q: Do all fibers within a motor unit contract together?
A: Yes.

Q: What is a muscle twitch?
A: A brief contraction from a single action potential.

Q: The three phases of a muscle twitch?
A: Latent, contraction, and relaxation.

Q: Twitch phase where the signal fires and EC coupling occurs?
A: The latent period.

Q: Twitch phase where Ca2+ binds troponin and crossbridges form?
A: The contraction period.

Q: Twitch phase where Ca2+ is pumped back and tension falls?
A: The relaxation period.

Q: What is graded contraction?
A: Muscle tension controlled by many factors for smooth control.

Q: Four factors that determine muscle tension?
A: Stimulus frequency, fiber length, fiber diameter, and number/size of motor units.

Q: What is muscle tone?
A: A small, constant, involuntary contraction that maintains posture.

Q: Does muscle tone cause movement?
A: No.

Q: Muscle attachment site at the stable position?
A: The origin.

Q: Muscle attachment site at the movable position?
A: The insertion.

Q: During movement, the insertion pulls toward what?
A: The origin.

Q: Muscle that produces a motion?
A: The agonist.

Q: Muscle that produces the opposite motion at the same joint?
A: The antagonist.

Q: Contraction where tension is constant and length changes?
A: Isotonic.

Q: Contraction where length is constant and tension changes?
A: Isometric.

Q: Which contraction type produces movement?
A: Isotonic.

Q: Which contraction type holds a weight in place?
A: Isometric.

Q: The three types of skeletal muscle fiber?
A: Slow oxidative, fast oxidative-glycolytic, and fast glycolytic.

Q: Which fiber type has the highest fatigue resistance?
A: Slow oxidative.

Q: Which fiber type has the lowest fatigue resistance?
A: Fast glycolytic.

Q: Which fiber type has the largest diameter?
A: Fast glycolytic.

Q: Which fiber type has the smallest diameter?
A: Slow oxidative.

Q: Which fiber type has the highest myoglobin?
A: Slow oxidative.

Q: Do fast glycolytic fibers rely on oxygen?
A: No.

Q: What color are fast glycolytic fibers?
A: White.

Q: Why are fast glycolytic fibers white?
A: They lack myoglobin.

Q: What structures connect cardiac muscle cells?
A: Intercalated discs.

Q: What allows electrical coupling within intercalated discs?
A: Gap junctions.

Q: What type of respiration does cardiac muscle rely on exclusively?
A: Aerobic respiration.

Q: Why does cardiac muscle contain many mitochondria?
A: Because of its high oxygen demand.

Q: Cardiac muscle acts as a functional what?
A: A syncytium.

Q: Cardiac muscle's ability to generate its own action potential?
A: Autorhythmicity.

Q: In cardiac EC coupling, Ca2+ first enters through which receptor?
A: The L-type DHP receptor.

Q: In cardiac muscle, what does extracellular Ca2+ open?
A: The ryanodine receptor release channel.

Q: Is the cardiac muscle action potential long or short?
A: Long and slow.

Q: Shape of smooth muscle cells?
A: Spindle shaped.

Q: What replaces troponin in smooth muscle?
A: Calmodulin.

Q: Is smooth muscle contraction faster or slower than striated muscle?
A: Slower.

Q: What enzyme phosphorylates myosin in smooth muscle?
A: Myosin light chain kinase.

Q: Where does the Ca2+ for smooth muscle contraction come from?
A: The extracellular space.

Q: Does smooth muscle need T-tubules?
A: No.

Q: What nervous system regulates smooth muscle?
A: The autonomic nervous system.

Q: What releases neurotransmitter in smooth muscle (no axon terminal)?
A: Varicosities.

Q: Where are receptors located on a smooth muscle cell?
A: Over the entire cell surface.

Q: Smooth muscle form where fibers contract as one via gap junctions?
A: Single-unit.

Q: Smooth muscle form where fibers act independently?
A: Multi-unit.

Q: Resting potential of single-unit autorhythmic smooth muscle?
A: -50 mV.

Q: Autorhythmic smooth muscle potential driven by K+ out, then Ca2+ in?
A: The pacemaker potential.

Q: Autorhythmic potential that cycles but doesn't always reach an AP?
A: The slow-wave potential.

Q: What drives slow-wave potentials?
A: Na+/K+ pump activity.

Q: How many channel types are involved in smooth muscle EC coupling?
A: Six.

Q: What do all smooth muscle EC-coupling channels ultimately cause?
A: Ca2+ release from the SR.

Q: Increase in cell size and myofibril number?
A: Hypertrophy.

Q: Increase in the number of cells through cell division?
A: Hyperplasia.

Q: Which muscle type can undergo limited hyperplasia?
A: Smooth muscle.

Q: The three components of the cardiovascular system?
A: The pump (heart), the tubes (vessels), and the fluid (blood).

Q: Circuit: right heart → lungs → left heart?
A: Pulmonary circulation.

Q: Circuit: left heart → organs → right heart?
A: Systemic circulation.

Q: Order of vessels from artery to vein?
A: Arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins.

Q: What is parallel flow?
A: Vessels run in parallel and are regulated independently.

Q: The outer covering of the heart?
A: The pericardium.

Q: Function of the pericardium?
A: Protects and anchors the heart.

Q: The two walls of the pericardium?
A: Visceral and parietal.

Q: How many chambers does the heart have?
A: Four.

Q: The two upper heart chambers?
A: The atria.

Q: The two lower heart chambers?
A: The ventricles.

Q: Which side of the heart handles pulmonary circulation?
A: The right side.

Q: Which side of the heart handles systemic circulation?
A: The left side.

Q: What do the atria do?
A: Receive blood from the great vessels.

Q: What do the ventricles do?
A: Pump blood out to the great vessels.

Q: Which ventricle is thicker?
A: The left ventricle.

Q: What do heart valves ensure?
A: One-way blood flow.

Q: Valves between the atria and ventricles?
A: The AV valves.

Q: What is another name for the left AV valve?
A: The bicuspid valve.

Q: What is another name for the right AV valve?
A: The tricuspid valve.

Q: The bicuspid valve is on which side of the heart?

Q: The tricuspid valve is on which side of the heart?
A: The right side.

Q: Valves between the ventricles and major arteries?
A: The semilunar valves.

Q: The semilunar valve on the left side?
A: The aortic valve.

Q: The semilunar valve on the right side?

Q: What supports the heart valves?
A: The fibrous skeleton.

Q: How many rings make up the fibrous skeleton?
A: Four.

Q: What supplies blood to the heart itself?

Q: Path of blood through the heart, starting at the right atrium?

Q: The two types of cardiac muscle fibers?
A: Pacemaker and contractile fibers.

Q: What does the cardiac conduction system do?
A: Initiates the action potential and coordinates contraction.

Q: Where does the conduction system start?
A: In the superior wall of the right atrium.

Q: Order of the cardiac conduction system?
A: SA node → AV node → AV bundle → bundle branches → Purkinje fibers.

Q: What does the AV bundle connect?
A: The atria to the ventricles.

Q: First node of the cardiac conduction system?
A: The SA node.

Q: Last component of the cardiac conduction system?
A: The Purkinje fibers.

Q: Threshold range of the pacemaker potential?
A: -60 mV to -40 mV.

Q: Ion sequence during the pacemaker potential?
A: K+ leaves, then Na+ enters, then Ca2+ enters.

Q: What sodium channel is involved in the cardiac pacemaker potential?
A: The F-type sodium channel.

Q: Do contractile cells depolarize on their own?
A: No.

Q: How many phases are in the contractile cell action potential?
A: Four.

Q: Contractile AP phase driven by Na+ entering?
A: Depolarization.

Q: Contractile AP phase driven by K+ exiting (early)?
A: Initial repolarization.

Q: Contractile AP phase where Ca2+ entry balances K+ exit?
A: The plateau.

Q: Contractile AP phase where Ca2+ closes and K+ exits?
A: Final repolarization.

Q: What ion causes the plateau in the cardiac action potential?
A: Ca2+.

Q: What causes the long refractory period in cardiac muscle?
A: The plateau.

Q: Does tetanic contraction occur in cardiac muscle?
A: No.

Q: Why can't the heart undergo tetanic contraction?
A: Because of the long refractory period.

Q: What kind of respiration is the heart entirely dependent on?
A: Aerobic respiration.

Q: What does an electrocardiogram record?
A: Cardiac muscle action potentials.

Q: What does the P-wave represent?
A: Atrial depolarization.

Q: What does the P-R interval represent?
A: AV node delay.

Q: What does the QRS complex represent?
A: Ventricular depolarization.

Q: What does the S-T segment represent?
A: The ventricles staying depolarized.

Q: What does the T-wave represent?
A: Ventricular repolarization.

Q: How many phases are in the cardiac cycle?
A: Five.

Q: Cardiac cycle phase 1?
A: Passive ventricular filling.

Q: Cardiac cycle phase 2?
A: Atrial contraction.

Q: Cardiac cycle phase 3?
A: Isovolumetric ventricular contraction.

Q: Cardiac cycle phase 4?
A: Ventricular ejection.

Q: Cardiac cycle phase 5?
A: Isovolumetric ventricular relaxation.

Q: During passive ventricular filling, which valves are open?
A: The AV valves (semilunar closed).

Q: During isovolumetric ventricular contraction, which valves are open?
A: None; all valves are closed.

Q: During ventricular ejection, which valves are open?
A: The semilunar valves (AV closed).

Q: During isovolumetric ventricular relaxation, which valves are open?
A: None; all valves are closed.

Q: What causes the first heart sound (S1)?
A: Closure of the AV valves.

Q: What causes the second heart sound (S2)?
A: Closure of the semilunar valves.

Q: Which ECG wave coincides with the second heart sound (S2)?
A: The T-wave.

Q: Which heart sound is louder and longer?
A: S1.

Q: Which heart sound is shorter and quieter?
A: S2.

Q: What is cardiac output?
A: The amount of blood ejected per minute.

Q: Equation for cardiac output?
A: CO = stroke volume × heart rate.

Q: The three factors of stroke volume?
A: Preload, contractility, and afterload.

Q: What is preload?
A: The stretch on the heart from blood filling it.

Q: Law stating that more filling before contraction gives more force?
A: The Frank-Starling law.

Q: What is contractility?
A: The force of contraction.

Q: Drugs that shift contractility?
A: Inotropic agents.

Q: How does a positive inotropic agent affect contraction and Ca2+?
A: It increases both.

Q: How does a negative inotropic agent work?
A: It decreases contraction by raising K+ or blocking Ca2+.

Q: What is afterload?
A: The pressure that must be overcome before the valve can open.

Q: High afterload means how much blood is pumped?
A: Less.

Q: What controls heart rate?
A: The autonomic nervous system.

Q: Which node do sympathetic and parasympathetic regulation act on?
A: The SA node.

Q: What is an arrhythmia?
A: A deviation from normal heart rhythm.

Q: What is a STEMI?

Q: What drug limits clot growth during a STEMI?
A: Aspirin.

Q: What drug dilates coronary arteries and veins during a STEMI?
A: Nitroglycerin.

Q: What drug is used for cardiac arrest?
A: Epinephrine.

Q: The outermost vessel layer?

Q: Function of capillaries?

Q: Groups of capillaries within a tissue are called?

Q: What do mid-size veins contain to prevent backflow?

Q: Where is most of the body's blood held (reservoir)?
A: Veins and venules (64%).

Q: Which structures hold the least blood?

Q: The three mechanisms of capillary exchange?
A: Diffusion, transcytosis, and bulk flow.

Q: How do proteins and large hormones cross the capillary wall?

Q: Bulk flow out of capillaries into interstitial fluid?

Q: Bulk flow from interstitial fluid into capillaries?

Q: Which way does interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure pull fluid?
