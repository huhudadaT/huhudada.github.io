Description: Mendelian inheritance, gene interactions and epistasis, sex linkage, and linkage mapping with tetrad analysis.
Class: BIOSC 0350
Semester: Summer 2026

Q: What is a gene?
A: A unit of heredity that determines a trait.

Q: What is an allele?
A: An alternative version of a gene.

Q: What is a locus?
A: The specific physical location of a gene on a chromosome.

Q: What is the difference between genotype and phenotype?
A: Genotype is the genetic makeup (alleles); phenotype is the observable expression.

Q: What does homozygous mean?
A: Having two identical alleles at a locus.

Q: What does heterozygous mean?
A: Having two different alleles at a locus.

Q: What does hemizygous mean?
A: Having only one allele at a locus due to having a single copy of that chromosome.

Q: Why are XY males hemizygous for X-linked genes?
A: They have only one X chromosome, so only one allele of each X-linked gene.

Q: What is a true-breeding line?
A: A line whose offspring always display the same trait when selfed or intercrossed.

Q: Are true-breeding organisms homozygous or heterozygous?
A: Homozygous.

Q: What is a wild-type allele?
A: An allele present at greater than 1% frequency in a population.

Q: What is a mutant allele?
A: A rare allele arising from mutation, present at less than 1% in a population.

Q: How do new alleles arise?
A: Through mutations (insertions, substitutions, deletions) in DNA sequence.

Q: What is a monohybrid cross?
A: A cross between two heterozygotes at one gene (Aa × Aa).

Q: What is a dihybrid cross?
A: A cross between two heterozygotes at two genes (AaBb × AaBb).

Q: What is a testcross?
A: A cross of an individual showing the dominant phenotype to a homozygous recessive individual.

Q: What is the purpose of a testcross?
A: To determine the unknown genotype of an individual showing the dominant phenotype.

Q: What is a reciprocal cross?
A: A cross repeated with the sexes of the parents swapped relative to the alleles they carry.

Q: What is a character?
A: The type of trait being studied (e.g., seed color).

Q: What is a trait?
A: A specific form of a character (e.g., yellow or green).

Q: What is a discrete trait?
A: A trait that falls into clear either/or categories.

Q: What is a continuous trait?
A: A trait that varies over a range, usually controlled by many genes plus environment.

Q: List Mendel's three main reasons for success.
A: Careful experimental design, use of true-breeding lines, and choice of the right model organism and traits.

Q: Why was Pisum sativum a good model organism?
A: It is diploid (not polyploid) and has discrete, monogenic traits.

Q: State Mendel's Law of Segregation.
A: The two alleles of each gene separate during meiosis, so each gamete receives only one allele.

Q: State Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment.
A: Alleles of different genes assort into gametes independently of one another.

Q: What is the chromosomal basis of segregation?
A: Homologous chromosomes separate at anaphase I of meiosis.

Q: What is the chromosomal basis of independent assortment?
A: Random alignment of homologous pairs at metaphase I.

Q: For genes on different chromosomes, how many possible combinations of maternal/paternal chromosomes are in gametes from n pairs?
A: 2^n.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross with complete dominance?
A: 3:1.

Q: F2 genotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross?
A: 1:2:1.

Q: F1 phenotype in a monohybrid cross with complete dominance?
A: All dominant phenotype.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross with incomplete dominance?
A: 1:2:1.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross with codominance?
A: 1:2:1.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio when one allele is a recessive lethal?
A: 2:1.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio of a dihybrid cross with no interaction?
A: 9:3:3:1.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio for recessive epistasis?
A: 9:3:4.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio for reciprocal recessive epistasis?
A: 9:7.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio for dominant epistasis I?
A: 12:3:1.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio for dominant epistasis II?
A: 13:3.

Q: F2 phenotypic ratio for redundant genes?
A: 15:1.

Q: Ratio of progeny from a testcross of a heterozygote × homozygous recessive?
A: 1:1.

Q: What is the product rule?
A: P(1 AND 2) = P(1) × P(2) for independent events.

Q: What is the sum rule?
A: P(1 OR 2) = P(1) + P(2) for mutually exclusive events.

Q: Probability that an unaffected child of two carriers is a carrier?
A: 2/3.

Q: Why is the answer 2/3 instead of 1/2 for the carrier probability?
A: Because the affected (aa) class is excluded; of the three unaffected outcomes (AA, Aa, Aa), two are carriers.

Q: What is incomplete dominance?
A: Heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype between the two homozygotes.

Q: What is codominance?
A: Heterozygotes display both parental phenotypes simultaneously.

Q: Example of incomplete dominance?
A: Red × white snapdragons producing pink F1.

Q: Example of codominance?
A: ABO blood types (I^A I^B individuals have both A and B antigens).

Q: What is the dominance hierarchy of ABO alleles?
A: I^A = I^B > i.

Q: Blood type of I^A I^B individual?
A: Type AB.

Q: Blood type of ii individual?
A: Type O.

Q: What is an allelic series?
A: Multiple alleles of the same gene ordered by dominance.

Q: Allelic series for rabbit coat color?
A: C > c^ch > c^h > c (full > chinchilla > himalayan > albino).

Q: How many alleles of a gene does any single individual carry?
A: Two (one on each homolog).

Q: What is a recessive lethal allele?
A: A loss-of-function allele of an essential gene; homozygotes die.

Q: Example of a recessive lethal that is dominant for a visible phenotype?
A: The A^Y (yellow) allele in mice.

Q: What is a conditional lethal allele?
A: An allele that is lethal only under specific environmental (restrictive) conditions.

Q: Example of a conditional lethal?
A: Shibire mutants in Drosophila — viable below 29°C, lethal above.

Q: What are permissive vs. restrictive conditions?
A: Permissive = mutant allele functions normally; restrictive = mutant allele is defective.

Q: What is pleiotropy?
A: One gene affecting multiple, apparently unrelated phenotypic traits.

Q: Example of pleiotropy?
A: The e4 allele of Apo-E increases risk of both cardiac disease and Alzheimer's.

Q: How is pleiotropy distinguished from two independent genes?
A: Pleiotropy is one locus affecting multiple traits; two genes are two separate loci.

Q: What is penetrance?
A: The proportion of individuals with a genotype who display the expected phenotype.

Q: What is expressivity?
A: The degree or intensity with which a phenotype is expressed.

Q: What is incomplete penetrance?
A: When some individuals with the genotype do not show the phenotype.

Q: What is variable expressivity?
A: When the phenotype varies in severity between affected individuals.

Q: What are modifier genes?
A: Other loci whose alleles influence the expressivity of the gene of interest.

Q: What is a complex trait?
A: A trait controlled by many genes plus the environment.

Q: Define a necessary condition.
A: X must be present for Y to occur.

Q: Define a sufficient condition.
A: X alone guarantees Y.

Q: How do you prove a factor is necessary?
A: Find a case where it is absent and the outcome fails.

Q: How do you prove a factor is sufficient?
A: Find a case where it is present and the outcome occurs.

Q: Is SRY necessary for morphological maleness?
A: Yes — XY individuals without functional SRY develop as female.

Q: Is SRY sufficient for morphological maleness?
A: Yes — XX individuals with translocated SRY develop as male.

Q: What is additive gene interaction?
A: Each combination of dominant/recessive alleles produces a distinct phenotype (9:3:3:1).

Q: What is recessive epistasis?
A: Homozygous recessive of one gene masks both alleles of the other gene.

Q: Example of recessive epistasis?
A: Labrador retriever coat color (ee masks B locus, giving yellow).

Q: What is reciprocal recessive epistasis?
A: Homozygous recessive of either gene masks the dominant allele of the other.

Q: Example of reciprocal recessive epistasis?
A: Sweet pea flower color (9 purple : 7 white).

Q: What is dominant epistasis I?
A: Dominant allele of one gene masks both alleles of the other gene.

Q: Example of dominant epistasis I?
A: Summer squash color (12:3:1).

Q: What is dominant epistasis II?
A: Dominant allele of one gene masks the dominant allele of the other.

Q: Example of dominant epistasis II?
A: Chicken feather color (13:3).

Q: What are redundant genes?
A: Two genes that perform the same function in parallel; only one dominant allele is needed.

Q: Example of redundant genes?
A: Maize leaf development (15:1).

Q: What is a complementation test?
A: A cross of two homozygous mutant strains to determine if mutations are in the same or different genes.

Q: Complementation test result if progeny are wild-type?
A: Mutations are in different genes (complementation).

Q: Complementation test result if progeny are mutant?
A: Mutations are in the same gene (no complementation).

Q: What is locus heterogeneity?
A: A phenotype caused by mutations in any one of multiple different genes.

Q: How do you identify the gene interaction from F2 data?
A: Divide total progeny by 16, then divide each class by that number to match a diagnostic ratio.

Q: What is a sister chromatid?
A: One of two identical copies of a replicated chromosome joined at the centromere.

Q: What are homologous chromosomes?
A: Chromosomes that match in size, shape, and gene content but may carry different alleles.

Q: What are nonsister chromatids?
A: Chromatids belonging to homologous chromosomes (not the same chromosome).

Q: Which chromatids exchange material in crossing over?
A: Nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.

Q: What are nonhomologous chromosomes?
A: Chromosomes carrying different genes.

Q: What is the centromere?
A: The constricted region holding sister chromatids together.

Q: What is the kinetochore?
A: A protein complex at the centromere that attaches the chromosome to spindle microtubules.

Q: What is a metacentric chromosome?
A: A chromosome with the centromere near the middle.

Q: What is an acrocentric chromosome?
A: A chromosome with the centromere near (but not at) one end.

Q: What is a karyotype?
A: An organized display of chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs by size.

Q: How many autosome pairs do humans have?
A: 22.

Q: How do you count chromosomes in a cell?
A: Count centromeres.

Q: Number of chromosomes in a human cell in G1?
A: 46.

Q: Number of chromatids per chromosome in G1?
A: 1.

Q: Number of chromatids per chromosome in G2?
A: 2.

Q: When during the cell cycle does DNA replication occur?
A: S phase.

Q: What happens during G0?
A: The cell is quiescent (non-dividing).

Q: Purpose of mitosis?
A: Produce two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.

Q: Purpose of meiosis?
A: Produce four genetically distinct haploid gametes.

Q: What separates during mitotic anaphase?
A: Sister chromatids.

Q: What separates during anaphase I?
A: Homologous chromosomes.

Q: What separates during anaphase II?
A: Sister chromatids.

Q: Which meiotic division reduces ploidy?
A: Meiosis I.

Q: Why is meiosis I the reduction division?
A: Homologous chromosomes separate, dropping ploidy from 2n to n.

Q: What is synapsis?
A: Pairing of homologous chromosomes during prophase I.

Q: What is the synaptonemal complex?
A: The protein structure that mediates synapsis between homologs.

Q: What is a bivalent (or tetrad)?
A: A pair of synapsed homologous chromosomes.

Q: What is a chiasma?
A: The site where crossing over occurs between nonsister chromatids.

Q: At what stage do homologous chromosomes pair?
A: Prophase I.

Q: At what stage does crossing over occur?
A: Prophase I.

Q: How many DNA replication events occur before meiosis?
A: One (before meiosis I).

Q: Does DNA replicate between meiosis I and meiosis II?
A: No.

Q: What protein holds sister chromatids together?
A: Cohesin.

Q: What protein cleaves cohesin at anaphase?
A: Separase.

Q: What is nondisjunction?
A: Failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate properly during cell division.

Q: What is aneuploidy?
A: An abnormal chromosome number (e.g., trisomy or monosomy).

Q: What fails to separate in MI nondisjunction?
A: Homologous chromosomes.

Q: What fails to separate in MII nondisjunction?
A: Sister chromatids.

Q: Gamete outcomes from MI nondisjunction in a heterozygous Aa individual?
A: 2 gametes are n+1 (with both A and a), 2 are n-1.

Q: Gamete outcomes from MII nondisjunction in a heterozygous Aa individual?
A: 2 normal n gametes, 1 n+1 with two identical alleles (AA or aa), 1 n-1.

Q: How can you tell MI vs. MII nondisjunction from allele data?
A: MI gives n+1 gametes with two different alleles; MII gives n+1 with two identical alleles.

Q: Why is homologous recombination required for proper MI segregation?
A: Chiasmata physically hold homologs together so they segregate to opposite poles.

Q: What aneuploidy causes Down syndrome?
A: Trisomy 21.

Q: Karyotype of Turner syndrome?
A: XO.

Q: Karyotype of Klinefelter syndrome?
A: XXY.

Q: Sex of XXY individual?
A: Male (has SRY).

Q: Sex of XO individual?
A: Female.

Q: What does SRY stand for?
A: Sex-determining Region of the Y chromosome.

Q: Function of SRY?
A: Directs the embryonic gonad to develop into testes.

Q: How do X and Y chromosomes pair reliably in meiosis?
A: They pair at pseudoautosomal regions (PARs).

Q: Are X and Y fully homologous?
A: No — they share only short pseudoautosomal regions.

Q: Why are X-linked recessive traits more common in males?
A: Males are hemizygous; one recessive allele on their single X is enough.

Q: Surviving sex ratio when an X-linked recessive lethal is in play (X^A X^a × X^A Y)?
A: 2 females : 1 male.

Q: What is a sex-limited trait?
A: A trait expressed in only one sex.

Q: What is a sex-influenced trait?
A: A trait that appears in both sexes but is expressed differently.

Q: Sex determination in birds?
A: ZW system (females ZW, males ZZ).

Q: Sex determination in Drosophila?
A: X-to-autosome ratio (not Y presence).

Q: Pedigree symbol for affected female?
A: Filled circle.

Q: Pedigree symbol for unaffected male?
A: Open square.

Q: Inheritance pattern for vertical pedigree pattern?
A: Autosomal dominant.

Q: Inheritance pattern for horizontal pedigree pattern (skips generations)?
A: Autosomal recessive.

Q: Key feature of autosomal recessive pedigrees?
A: Affected individuals often have unaffected (carrier) parents.

Q: Key feature of autosomal dominant pedigrees?
A: Every affected person has at least one affected parent.

Q: Example of autosomal dominant disease?
A: Huntington disease.

Q: Example of autosomal recessive disease?
A: Cystic fibrosis.

Q: Key feature of X-linked recessive pedigrees?
A: Males affected far more often than females.

Q: Can an affected father pass X-linked recessive to his son?
A: No — sons inherit Y from father.

Q: Daughters of an affected X-linked recessive male are what?
A: Obligate carriers.

Q: Example of X-linked recessive disease?
A: Hemophilia.

Q: Key feature of X-linked dominant?
A: Affected males pass it to all daughters but no sons.

Q: Key feature of Y-linked inheritance?
A: Only males affected; passed father to son.

Q: Key feature of mitochondrial inheritance?
A: Passed only from mother to all offspring.

Q: What is linkage?
A: Two genes on the same chromosome tending to be inherited together.

Q: What are parental gametes?
A: Gametes with allele combinations matching the parental chromosomes.

Q: What are recombinant gametes?
A: Gametes with allele combinations different from parental chromosomes (due to crossover).

Q: What is cis configuration?
A: Both dominant alleles on the same homolog (AB/ab).

Q: What is trans configuration?
A: Dominant alleles on different homologs (Ab/aB).

Q: How do you identify parentals in a testcross?
A: They are the two most frequent progeny classes.

Q: Formula for recombination frequency?
A: RF = (# recombinants / total progeny) × 100.

Q: Maximum value of recombination frequency?
A: 50%.

Q: Why is the maximum RF 50%?
A: Crossing over at the four-strand stage produces only 50% recombinant chromatids per meiosis.

Q: What does 1% RF equal?
A: 1 map unit (mu) or 1 centimorgan (cM).

Q: RF below 50% indicates what?
A: The genes are linked.

Q: RF near 50% indicates what?
A: Genes are unlinked (different chromosomes or very far apart).

Q: In a three-point cross, which classes are most frequent?
A: Parental (no crossover) classes.

Q: In a three-point cross, which classes are least frequent?
A: Double crossover (DCO) classes.

Q: How do you find the middle gene in a three-point cross?
A: Compare DCO to parental; the allele that "swaps" identifies the middle gene.

Q: Formula for map distance in a three-point cross interval?
A: (SCOs in interval + DCOs) / total × 100.

Q: Why are DCOs counted in both adjacent intervals?
A: A double crossover involves one crossover in each interval.

Q: Formula for expected number of DCOs?
A: RF1 × RF2 × total progeny.

Q: Formula for coefficient of coincidence?
A: coc = DCOs observed / DCOs expected.

Q: Formula for interference?
A: I = 1 − coc.

Q: What does positive interference mean?
A: A crossover in one region inhibits crossovers nearby.

Q: At what stage does crossing over occur?
A: The four-strand stage (after replication) of prophase I.

Q: Why is genetic distance different from physical distance?
A: Recombination frequency varies by region (hotspots and coldspots), not just nucleotide distance.

Q: Null hypothesis for a chi-square test of linkage?
A: The two genes assort independently.

Q: Formula for chi-square?
A: χ² = Σ[(O − E)² / E].

Q: Formula for degrees of freedom?
A: df = number of classes − 1.

Q: P-value threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis?
A: P < 0.05.

Q: What does P < 0.05 mean in a linkage test?
A: Reject the null; evidence supports linkage.

Q: What does P ≥ 0.05 mean in a linkage test?
A: Fail to reject the null; no evidence for linkage.

Q: Can a chi-square test prove the null hypothesis?
A: No — it can only fail to reject it.

Q: Expected counts in a test cross of 200 progeny under independent assortment (2 classes)?
A: 100 parental and 100 recombinant.

Q: How do you count minimum crossovers needed to produce a gamete?
A: Count the number of switches between homologs along the chromosome.

Q: Minimum crossovers if a gamete matches a parental chromosome exactly?
A: Zero.

Q: What is a parental ditype (PD) tetrad?
A: A tetrad with four parental-type spores.

Q: What is a nonparental ditype (NPD) tetrad?
A: A tetrad with four recombinant-type spores.

Q: What is a tetratype (T) tetrad?
A: A tetrad with all four different spore genotypes.

Q: Rule for unlinked genes in tetrad analysis?
A: PD ≈ NPD.

Q: Rule for linked genes in tetrad analysis?
A: PD >> NPD.

Q: Formula for RF from tetrad data?
A: RF = [(NPD + ½T) / total tetrads] × 100.

Q: Formula for gene-to-centromere distance in ordered tetrads?
A: [(½ × # MII tetrads) / total tetrads] × 100.
