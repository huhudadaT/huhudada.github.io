Q: Which alveolar cell type secretes surfactant?
A. Type I alveolar cells
B. Alveolar macrophages
*C. Type II alveolar cells
D. Goblet cells
Explain: Type II cells secrete surfactant to lower surface tension; Type I cells are the thin cells where gas exchange occurs.

Q: Compared with the systemic circuit, the pulmonary circuit is characterized by:
*A. High flow and low pressure
B. High flow and high pressure
C. Low flow and high pressure
D. Low flow and low pressure
Explain: The pulmonary circuit receives the entire cardiac output (high flow) but runs at low pressure (~25/8 mmHg).

Q: Sensory axons from the carotid bodies reach the brainstem via which cranial nerve?
A. Vagus (X)
B. Phrenic
C. Facial (VII)
*D. Glossopharyngeal (IX)
Explain: The carotid bodies are innervated by the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX); the aortic bodies use the vagus (X).

Q: The aortic bodies send their sensory information to the brainstem through the:
A. Glossopharyngeal nerve (IX)
*B. Vagus nerve (X)
C. Intercostal nerves
D. Hypoglossal nerve (XII)
Explain: Aortic body afferents run in the vagus (X); carotid body afferents run in the glossopharyngeal (IX).

Q: During inspiration the diaphragm contracts and thoracic volume increases. Alveolar pressure therefore:
A. Rises above atmospheric pressure
B. Stays equal to atmospheric pressure
*C. Falls below atmospheric pressure
D. Becomes equal to intrapleural pressure
Explain: By Boyle's law, increased volume lowers alveolar pressure below atmospheric, drawing air in.

Q: All of the following are true of cystic fibrosis EXCEPT:
*A. It primarily impairs cardiac muscle contraction
B. It results from a mutation in the CFTR gene
C. Chloride fails to exit the cell
D. It produces abnormally thick mucus
Explain: CF affects secretory epithelia (lung, pancreas, etc.) through defective Cl- transport; it is not a primary cardiac muscle disorder.

Q: A patient with emphysema has lost lung elastic recoil. This corresponds to:
A. Low compliance
B. High airway resistance
C. Increased alveolar surface tension
*D. High compliance
Explain: Emphysema raises compliance (easy to inflate, poor recoil); fibrosis instead lowers compliance.

Q: By the Law of LaPlace (P = 2T/r), why would small alveoli tend to empty into larger ones without surfactant?
A. A smaller radius produces a lower collapsing pressure
*B. A smaller radius produces a higher collapsing pressure
C. Surface tension is zero in small alveoli
D. Large alveoli have higher surface tension
Explain: Smaller radius means higher pressure, so small alveoli would empty into large ones; surfactant lowers surface tension most in small alveoli to offset this.

Q: Which lung volume cannot be measured directly by spirometry?
A. Tidal volume
B. Inspiratory reserve volume
*C. Residual volume
D. Expiratory reserve volume
Explain: Residual volume is the air left after maximal exhalation and cannot be exhaled, so spirometry cannot capture it.

Q: Oxygen is carried on hemoglobin bound to which component?
*A. The iron in heme
B. The globin amino chains
C. Plasma albumin
D. The 2,3-BPG binding site
Explain: O2 binds the Fe in heme, whereas CO2 binds the amino (globin) chains, a separate site.

Q: In metabolically active tissue, rising CO2 and H+ shift the oxygen-hemoglobin curve to the right. This indicates hemoglobin's affinity for O2:
A. Increases, loading more O2
B. Is unchanged
C. Increases only at high altitude
*D. Decreases, unloading more O2
Explain: The Bohr effect: greater acidity/CO2 lowers O2 affinity, promoting unloading where tissues need oxygen.

Q: Fetal hemoglobin has a higher O2 affinity than adult hemoglobin because it:
A. Binds more 2,3-BPG
*B. Binds less 2,3-BPG
C. Contains more iron atoms per molecule
D. Has a higher P50
Explain: BPG lowers O2 affinity; because fetal Hb binds BPG poorly, its affinity is higher, favoring O2 transfer across the placenta.

Q: The largest fraction of CO2 in blood is transported as:
A. CO2 dissolved in plasma
B. Carbaminohemoglobin
*C. Bicarbonate ions
D. CO2 bound to plasma albumin
Explain: About 70% of CO2 travels as bicarbonate (HCO3-); the rest is carbamino compounds and dissolved gas.

Q: The central chemoreceptors of the medullary respiratory center respond primarily to:
*A. A rise in CO2 and H+
B. A fall in arterial O2
C. A rise in arterial O2
D. A fall in blood pressure
Explain: Central chemoreceptors track CO2/H+; sensing low O2 is the role of the peripheral (carotid and aortic) chemoreceptors.

Q: The dorsal respiratory group drives the diaphragm through the phrenic nerve, which arises from which spinal levels?
A. C1-C2
B. T1-T4
C. L1-L3
*D. C3-C5
Explain: "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive" - the phrenic nerve originates from C3 through C5.

Q: Carbon monoxide is dangerous largely because its affinity for hemoglobin is roughly:
A. Equal to that of O2
*B. 200 times that of O2
C. 20 times that of O2
D. One-tenth that of O2
Explain: CO binds hemoglobin about 200x more tightly than O2, blocking oxygen transport even at low concentrations.

Q: Which statement about gas exchange is NOT correct?
A. Dalton's law concerns the partial pressures of gases in a mixture
B. Henry's law concerns how much gas dissolves in a liquid
*C. During pulmonary gas exchange, O2 diffuses from the blood into the alveoli
D. CO2 is more soluble in plasma than O2
Explain: In pulmonary gas exchange O2 moves FROM the alveoli INTO the blood (and CO2 the reverse); the direction stated is backwards.

Q: Tubular secretion moves substances in which direction?
A. From the tubule into the blood
*B. From the blood into the tubule
C. From Bowman's capsule into the glomerulus
D. From the collecting duct into the renal artery
Explain: Secretion adds substances from blood into the tubular fluid; reabsorption is the opposite (tubule to blood).

Q: Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole and exits through the:
A. Afferent arteriole
B. Renal vein directly
C. Vasa recta
*D. Efferent arteriole
Explain: The efferent arteriole carries blood away from the glomerulus - an unusual arteriole-to-arteriole arrangement.

Q: The Na+/K+/2Cl- (NKCC) cotransporter is located in which nephron segment?
A. Thin descending limb of the loop
*B. Thick ascending limb of the loop
C. Proximal convoluted tubule
D. Collecting duct
Explain: NKCC reabsorbs ions in the thick ascending limb, which is impermeable to water; the descending limb reabsorbs water, not ions.

Q: Regarding the loop of Henle, which statement is FALSE?
A. The descending limb is permeable to water
B. The ascending limb reabsorbs ions but not water
C. The descending limb does not reabsorb ions
*D. The ascending limb is freely permeable to water
Explain: The ascending limb is impermeable to water and reabsorbs ions only - "water falls" out of the descending limb.

Q: Principal cells of the collecting duct reabsorb ___ and secrete ___.
*A. Na+ ; K+
B. K+ ; Na+
C. H+ ; HCO3-
D. HCO3- ; H+
Explain: Principal cells reabsorb Na+ and secrete K+; intercalated cells handle the H+/HCO3- exchange.

Q: Intercalated cells help acidify the urine by:
A. Reabsorbing H+
B. Reabsorbing K+
*C. Secreting H+
D. Secreting HCO3-
Explain: Intercalated cells secrete H+ (and reabsorb HCO3-), which is why urine can be up to ~1000x more acidic than blood.

Q: The principal renal action of aldosterone is to:
A. Decrease Na+ reabsorption and increase K+ reabsorption
*B. Increase Na+ reabsorption and increase K+ secretion
C. Increase water reabsorption by inserting aquaporins
D. Increase the glomerular filtration rate
Explain: Aldosterone drives Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion in the principal cells; water follows the Na+, raising blood volume and pressure.

Q: Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentrates the urine by:
A. Increasing Na+ reabsorption in the loop of Henle
B. Inhibiting renin release
*C. Inserting aquaporin-2 channels into principal cells
D. Dilating the afferent arteriole
Explain: ADH inserts aquaporin-2 into the principal-cell apical membrane, increasing facultative water reabsorption.

Q: Sympathetic stimulation of alpha-1 receptors on the renal arterioles produces:
*A. Vasoconstriction and a fall in GFR
B. Vasodilation and a rise in GFR
C. Renin release with no vascular effect
D. Increased aquaporin insertion
Explain: Alpha-1 activation constricts the arterioles and lowers GFR, while beta-1 activation triggers renin release.

Q: Which pressure is the primary force PROMOTING glomerular filtration?
A. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure
*B. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure
C. Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure
D. Blood urea nitrogen
Explain: Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid into Bowman's capsule; the other two pressures oppose filtration.

Q: A substance that is freely filtered but neither reabsorbed nor secreted, so that its clearance equals GFR, is:
A. Urea
B. PAH
C. Glucose
*D. Inulin
Explain: Inulin (not insulin) is the classic GFR marker; PAH is secreted (clearance greater than GFR) and urea is partly reabsorbed (clearance less than GFR).

Q: If a substance has a renal clearance greater than its GFR, it must be:
A. Reabsorbed on net
*B. Secreted on net
C. Neither reabsorbed nor secreted
D. Not filtered at all
Explain: Clearance above GFR means the tubules add the substance to the filtrate (net secretion), as with PAH.

Q: Which structure acts as the countercurrent exchanger that preserves the medullary osmotic gradient?
A. Loop of Henle
B. Collecting duct
*C. Vasa recta
D. Glomerulus
Explain: The vasa recta passively exchange water and solutes to maintain the gradient built by the loop's countercurrent multiplier.

Q: The presence of which substance in the urine is considered NORMAL?
A. Glucose
B. Albumin
C. Ketone bodies
*D. Small amounts of urea
Explain: Urea is a normal urinary waste product; urinary glucose, albumin, or ketones each indicate a pathology.

Q: Crossing over between non-sister chromatids occurs during:
*A. Prophase I
B. Prophase II
C. Anaphase I
D. Metaphase II
Explain: Crossing over (tetrad formation and recombination) happens in prophase I of meiosis I.

Q: According to the textbook, homologous chromosome pairs and sister chromatids separate during which divisions, respectively?
A. Homologs in meiosis II; sister chromatids in meiosis I
B. Both separate in meiosis I
*C. Homologs in meiosis I; sister chromatids in meiosis II
D. Both separate in meiosis II
Explain: Textbook 23.1 states homologs separate in anaphase I and sister chromatids separate in meiosis II. (Your lecture slide 7 says meiosis II for the homologs, so confirm which version your professor grades.)

Q: Which testicular cells produce testosterone?
A. Sertoli cells
*B. Leydig (interstitial) cells
C. Spermatogonia
D. Primary spermatocytes
Explain: Leydig cells make testosterone; Sertoli cells support sperm, form the blood-testis barrier, and secrete inhibin.

Q: All of the following are functions of Sertoli cells EXCEPT:
A. Forming the blood-testis barrier
B. Secreting inhibin
C. Secreting androgen-binding protein
*D. Producing testosterone
Explain: Testosterone is produced by Leydig cells; Sertoli cells nourish developing sperm and form the barrier.

Q: Erection and ejaculation are mediated, respectively, by which autonomic divisions?
*A. Erection = parasympathetic; ejaculation = sympathetic
B. Erection = sympathetic; ejaculation = parasympathetic
C. Both parasympathetic
D. Both sympathetic
Explain: "Point and shoot" - parasympathetic drives erection (via nitric oxide), sympathetic drives ejaculation.

Q: Which accessory gland contributes the largest share (~60-70%) of semen volume?
A. Prostate gland
*B. Seminal vesicles
C. Bulbourethral glands
D. Epididymis
Explain: The seminal vesicles supply ~60-70% (fructose-rich, alkaline); the prostate adds ~25% and the bulbourethral glands ~5%.

Q: The prostate gland secretes a fluid that is:
A. Alkaline and rich in fructose
B. Alkaline and released before ejaculation to lubricate the urethra
*C. Slightly acidic, containing citric acid and clot-breaking enzymes
D. The main source of clotting fibrinogen
Explain: Prostatic fluid is milky and slightly acidic with citric acid and enzymes (e.g., PSA) that liquefy the semen clot; fructose and fibrinogen come from the seminal vesicles.

Q: In human oogenesis, the secondary oocyte is released at ovulation while arrested in:
A. Prophase I
B. Metaphase I
C. Anaphase II
*D. Metaphase II
Explain: The primary oocyte is arrested in prophase I until puberty; the secondary oocyte arrests in metaphase II and completes meiosis only if fertilized.

Q: How many functional gametes result from one primary oocyte completing meiosis?
*A. One
B. Two
C. Three
D. Four
Explain: Female meiosis yields a single ovum plus polar bodies that degenerate; male meiosis yields four sperm.

Q: From outermost to innermost, the layers of the uterine wall are:
A. Endometrium, myometrium, perimetrium
*B. Perimetrium, myometrium, endometrium
C. Myometrium, perimetrium, endometrium
D. Perimetrium, endometrium, myometrium
Explain: Perimetrium (outer serosa), myometrium (middle muscle), endometrium (inner lining).

Q: Just before ovulation, a sustained high level of estrogen exerts what kind of feedback on the anterior pituitary?
A. Negative feedback, suppressing LH
B. No feedback effect
*C. Positive feedback, triggering the LH surge
D. Positive feedback acting on the ovary only
Explain: Sustained high estrogen switches to positive feedback and triggers the LH surge; at moderate levels estrogen is inhibitory.

Q: The main role of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in early pregnancy is to:
A. Trigger ovulation
*B. Rescue the corpus luteum so it keeps producing progesterone
C. Initiate menstruation
D. Stimulate spermatogenesis
Explain: hCG from the early placenta maintains the corpus luteum until the placenta itself makes enough progesterone.

Q: In a genetic male, the Wolffian (mesonephric) ducts and Müllerian ducts do what, respectively?
*A. Wolffian ducts form the vas deferens; Müllerian ducts regress
B. Wolffian ducts form the fallopian tubes; Müllerian ducts regress
C. Wolffian ducts form the vas deferens; Müllerian ducts form the uterus
D. Wolffian ducts regress; Müllerian ducts form the vas deferens
Explain: Under testosterone the Wolffian ducts become male ducts (vas deferens), while the Müllerian ducts regress in males.

Q: Which gene on the Y chromosome initiates development of the testes?
A. DHT
B. hCG
*C. SRY
D. CFTR
Explain: The SRY gene directs the indifferent gonad to become a testis; without it, ovaries develop.

Q: Immediately after a sperm penetrates the oocyte, what blocks additional sperm from entering?
A. The corona radiata thickens
*B. The zona pellucida hardens (zona reaction)
C. The acrosome reseals
D. The oocyte completes meiosis I
Explain: Cortical granule release hardens the zona pellucida to block polyspermy.

Q: In what order does a sperm penetrate the coverings of the oocyte?
*A. Corona radiata, then zona pellucida, then plasma membrane
B. Zona pellucida, then corona radiata, then plasma membrane
C. Plasma membrane, then zona pellucida, then corona radiata
D. Corona radiata, then plasma membrane, then zona pellucida
Explain: The sperm crosses the outer corona radiata, then the zona pellucida, then fuses with the oocyte plasma membrane.

Q: Which sequence correctly traces air from outside the body to the alveoli?
A. Nose, larynx, pharynx, trachea, bronchi, alveoli
*B. Nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, alveoli
C. Nose, pharynx, trachea, larynx, bronchi, alveoli
D. Nose, larynx, trachea, pharynx, bronchi, alveoli
Explain: Air passes nose to pharynx (shared with food) to larynx to trachea to bronchi to alveoli.

Q: The serous membrane directly covering the surface of the lung is the:
A. Parietal pleura
B. Pericardium
*C. Visceral pleura
D. Mediastinum
Explain: The visceral pleura clings to the lung surface; the parietal pleura lines the chest wall.

Q: The respiratory epithelium of the conducting zone protects the lungs mainly by:
*A. Trapping particles in mucus and sweeping them up with cilia
B. Carrying out gas exchange
C. Secreting surfactant
D. Producing red blood cells
Explain: The mucociliary escalator traps inhaled debris in mucus and cilia move it upward; the conducting zone itself does not exchange gas.

Q: During quiet breathing, expiration occurs mainly because:
A. The diaphragm actively contracts
B. The external intercostals contract
C. Intrapleural pressure becomes positive
*D. The stretched lungs passively recoil
Explain: Quiet expiration is passive - elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall drives air out without muscle effort.

Q: The primary muscle responsible for quiet inspiration is the:
*A. Diaphragm
B. Internal intercostals
C. Rectus abdominis
D. Sternocleidomastoid
Explain: The diaphragm (with the external intercostals) produces quiet inspiration; the others are accessory or expiratory muscles.

Q: Which pairing is correct?
A. Pneumothorax = lung collapse from a mucus plug
*B. Pneumothorax = air in the pleural space; atelectasis = collapse from blockage or failure to expand
C. Atelectasis = air entering the pleural space
D. The two terms mean exactly the same thing
Explain: Pneumothorax is air entering the pleural space (breaking the seal); atelectasis is collapse from an airway blockage or inability to expand.

Q: Under normal conditions, airway resistance is greatest at the:
A. Trachea
B. Primary bronchi
*C. Bronchioles
D. Alveoli
Explain: The small-diameter bronchioles are the main site of airway resistance and are regulated by smooth muscle.

Q: A normal overall ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) ratio in the lung is approximately:
A. 0.2
*B. 0.8
C. 1.5
D. 3.0
Explain: The ideal ratio is 1.0, but gravity makes the realistic whole-lung average about 0.8.

Q: The maximum volume of air that can be exhaled after a maximum inspiration is the:
A. Tidal volume
B. Functional residual capacity
*C. Vital capacity
D. Residual volume
Explain: Vital capacity is the largest voluntary exhalation after a full inspiration; residual volume remains behind and is not part of it.

Q: Minute ventilation equals:
A. Tidal volume divided by respiratory rate
B. Vital capacity times respiratory rate
C. Residual volume plus tidal volume
*D. Tidal volume times respiratory rate
Explain: Minute ventilation is the tidal volume multiplied by the number of breaths per minute.

Q: The Haldane effect states that:
*A. Deoxygenated hemoglobin carries CO2 more readily than oxygenated hemoglobin
B. Oxygenated hemoglobin carries CO2 more readily
C. Low pH increases O2 affinity
D. CO2 cannot be carried by hemoglobin
Explain: When hemoglobin releases O2 it binds CO2 and buffers H+ more effectively, boosting CO2 pickup in the tissues.

Q: The pneumotaxic center affects the dorsal respiratory group by:
A. Exciting it so the lungs fill completely
*B. Inhibiting it so the lungs do not overfill
C. Generating the basic respiratory rhythm
D. Sensing arterial oxygen
Explain: The pneumotaxic center inhibits the DRG (limiting inspiration), whereas the apneustic center excites it.

Q: The inflation (Hering-Breuer) reflex functions to:
A. Increase heart rate during exercise
B. Trigger coughing
*C. Prevent overinflation of the lungs
D. Stimulate surfactant release
Explain: Airway stretch receptors inhibit inspiration once the lungs are sufficiently inflated, protecting against overinflation.

Q: An obstructive lung disease (such as emphysema) primarily impairs the ability to:
*A. Move air out of the lungs
B. Move air into the lungs
C. Diffuse gases across the alveolar membrane
D. Produce surfactant
Explain: Obstructive disease blocks airflow out (expiratory limitation); restrictive disease limits airflow in (reduced expansion).

Q: Which of the following is NOT a function of the kidneys?
A. Regulating blood pH
B. Regulating blood volume and pressure
C. Producing hormones
*D. Producing bile for digestion
Explain: Bile is made by the liver; the kidneys excrete wastes and regulate ions, pH, volume, pressure, osmolarity, and secrete hormones.

Q: Which nephron type has long loops reaching deep into the medulla and is responsible for concentrating urine?
A. Cortical nephron
*B. Juxtamedullary nephron
C. Both types equally
D. Neither type
Explain: Juxtamedullary nephrons (~20%) have long loops that build the medullary gradient; the more numerous cortical nephrons have short loops.

Q: In the juxtaglomerular apparatus, renin is released by the:
A. Macula densa cells
B. Podocytes
*C. Juxtaglomerular (granular) cells
D. Mesangial cells
Explain: JG cells are modified smooth muscle that secrete renin; the macula densa senses NaCl in the tubular fluid.

Q: Reabsorption in which substances pass BETWEEN adjacent tubule cells through tight junctions is called:
*A. Paracellular reabsorption
B. Transcellular reabsorption
C. Facultative reabsorption
D. Secretion
Explain: Paracellular movement goes between cells; transcellular movement passes through the cells via membrane transporters.

Q: Water reabsorption that is regulated by ADH in the late distal tubule and collecting duct is described as:
A. Obligatory
*B. Facultative
C. Paracellular
D. Countercurrent
Explain: Facultative (optional, hormone-controlled) water reabsorption depends on ADH; obligatory reabsorption occurs automatically in the proximal tubule and descending limb.

Q: What fraction of filtered glucose and amino acids is normally reabsorbed in the proximal tubule?
A. About 25%
B. About 65%
*C. 100%
D. 0%
Explain: The proximal tubule reabsorbs essentially all filtered glucose and amino acids using Na+-coupled symporters.

Q: Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) affects the kidney by:
A. Increasing Na+ reabsorption
B. Inserting aquaporins
C. Releasing renin
*D. Inhibiting Na+ reabsorption and raising filtration surface area
Explain: ANP promotes Na+ (and water) loss and relaxes mesangial cells to increase filtration surface area, lowering blood volume.

Q: Parathyroid hormone (PTH) acts on the distal tubule to:
*A. Increase Ca2+ reabsorption
B. Decrease Ca2+ reabsorption
C. Increase K+ secretion
D. Insert aquaporin-2 channels
Explain: PTH raises blood calcium partly by increasing Ca2+ reabsorption in the early distal tubule.

Q: The countercurrent multiplier of the loop of Henle establishes:
A. An acidic tubular fluid
*B. A high osmotic gradient in the medulla
C. The glomerular filtration barrier
D. Renin release
Explain: The loop's opposing water and ion movements build the medullary osmotic gradient (up to ~1200 mOsm) needed to concentrate urine.

Q: Urea contributes to urine concentration by:
A. Being fully excreted with no reabsorption
B. Blocking aquaporins
*C. Diffusing from the collecting duct into the medullary interstitium
D. Being secreted in the proximal tubule
Explain: Urea recycles from the inner medullary collecting duct into the interstitium, reinforcing medullary hypertonicity.

Q: Alcohol increases urine output primarily by:
A. Inhibiting Na+ reabsorption
B. Increasing GFR directly
C. Blocking aldosterone
*D. Suppressing ADH secretion
Explain: Alcohol inhibits ADH release, reducing water reabsorption; caffeine instead inhibits Na+ reabsorption.

Q: Plasma creatinine is a useful marker of kidney function because it is produced at a steady rate from:
A. Dietary protein in the gut
*B. Creatine phosphate breakdown in skeletal muscle
C. Hemoglobin degradation
D. Bile pigments
Explain: Creatinine comes from muscle creatine phosphate at a constant rate, so rising plasma levels signal reduced filtration.

Q: Urine formed in the kidney reaches the exterior by traveling through which sequence?
*A. Ureters, urinary bladder, urethra
B. Urethra, bladder, ureters
C. Bladder, ureters, urethra
D. Ureters, urethra, bladder
Explain: Urine drains from kidney to ureters to the bladder (storage) to the urethra; micturition is the reflex emptying of the bladder.

Q: Kidney stones most commonly form when which substance crystallizes out of the urine?
A. Glucose
B. Urea
*C. Calcium
D. Albumin
Explain: Excess solutes - most often calcium salts - can crystallize in the urinary tract to form stones.

Q: Hemodialysis is used in renal failure to:
A. Stimulate the kidneys to make more urine
*B. Filter wastes from the blood across an artificial membrane
C. Replace lost red blood cells
D. Increase renin secretion
Explain: When the kidneys fail (acute or chronic), hemodialysis clears wastes and excess fluid from the blood externally.

Q: The filtration slits of the renal corpuscle are formed by the foot processes (pedicels) of which cells?
A. Mesangial cells
B. Endothelial cells
*C. Podocytes
D. Juxtaglomerular cells
Explain: Podocytes wrap the glomerular capillaries; their interdigitating pedicels leave slits that restrict large molecules.

Q: Besides the kidneys, which organ also excretes metabolic waste (removing CO2)?
A. Pancreas
B. Spleen
C. Thyroid
*D. Lungs
Explain: Wastes leave the body by several routes - lungs (CO2), liver/GI tract, and sweat glands - not only the kidneys.

Q: A human somatic cell contains ___ chromosomes, while a gamete contains ___.
A. 23 (n); 46 (2n)
*B. 46 (2n); 23 (n)
C. 46 (2n); 46 (2n)
D. 23 (n); 23 (n)
Explain: Somatic cells are diploid (46, 2n); gametes are haploid (23, n) after meiosis.

Q: The scrotum holds the testes outside the body cavity because spermatogenesis requires a temperature:
A. Equal to core body temperature
B. About 3 degrees above core temperature
*C. About 3 degrees below core temperature
D. Independent of temperature
Explain: Sperm production needs a temperature roughly 3 C below core body temperature, which the scrotum maintains.

Q: Meiosis of one primary spermatocyte ultimately produces how many spermatids?
A. One
B. Two
C. Three
*D. Four
Explain: A primary spermatocyte completes meiosis I and II to yield four haploid spermatids (versus one ovum in females).

Q: Spermiogenesis is the process in which:
*A. Spermatids mature into motile sperm, gaining an acrosome and flagellum
B. Spermatogonia divide by mitosis
C. Primary spermatocytes undergo meiosis I
D. Sperm are stored in the epididymis
Explain: Spermiogenesis is the final remodeling of round spermatids into streamlined, tailed sperm cells.

Q: The acrosome of a sperm functions to:
A. Provide ATP for movement
*B. Release enzymes that penetrate the oocyte's coverings
C. Propel the sperm forward
D. Store maternal mitochondria
Explain: The acrosome caps the sperm head and releases enzymes to digest through the corona radiata and zona pellucida; the midpiece holds mitochondria and the tail provides motility.

Q: Where do sperm complete maturation and gain motility before ejaculation?
A. Seminiferous tubules
B. Vas deferens
*C. Epididymis
D. Prostatic urethra
Explain: Sperm leaving the testis are immotile and finish maturing (and are stored) in the epididymis.

Q: Which erectile body of the penis surrounds the urethra?
*A. Corpus spongiosum
B. Corpus cavernosum
C. Tunica albuginea
D. Glans epididymis
Explain: The single corpus spongiosum encloses the urethra; the two corpora cavernosa are the main erectile columns.

Q: During erection, parasympathetic nerves cause vasodilation of the penile arteries chiefly by releasing:
A. Norepinephrine
B. Acetylcholine acting directly on the muscle
*C. Nitric oxide, which raises cGMP
D. Testosterone
Explain: Nitric oxide raises cGMP, relaxing arterial smooth muscle so blood fills the erectile tissue; Viagra blocks the enzyme that degrades cGMP.

Q: In the male, luteinizing hormone (LH) primarily stimulates which cells to produce testosterone?
A. Sertoli cells
*B. Leydig cells
C. Spermatogonia
D. Anterior pituitary cells
Explain: LH targets Leydig cells (testosterone); FSH and testosterone act on Sertoli cells, which make androgen-binding protein and inhibin.

Q: Which of the following is NOT a normal effect of testosterone?
A. Development of male sex characteristics
B. Support of spermatogenesis
C. Anabolic, tissue-building effects
*D. Suppression of protein synthesis
Explain: Testosterone is anabolic and drives male characteristics and spermatogenesis; it does not suppress protein synthesis.

Q: Which set of hormones is secreted by the ovaries?
*A. Estrogens, progesterone, inhibin, and relaxin
B. Testosterone and LH
C. FSH and LH
D. ADH and oxytocin
Explain: The ovaries produce estrogens, progesterone, inhibin, and relaxin; FSH and LH come from the anterior pituitary.

Q: The blood-testis barrier, formed by Sertoli cells, functions to:
A. Produce testosterone
*B. Shield developing sperm from the immune system
C. Store mature sperm
D. Secrete seminal fluid
Explain: Because sperm form after immune tolerance is set, the Sertoli-cell barrier keeps them from being attacked as foreign.

Q: Place the earliest events of development in the correct order:
A. Cleavage, fertilization, blastocyst, morula, implantation
B. Fertilization, morula, cleavage, blastocyst, implantation
*C. Fertilization, cleavage, morula, blastocyst, implantation
D. Fertilization, blastocyst, morula, cleavage, implantation
Explain: A fertilized egg cleaves into a solid morula, cavitates into a blastocyst, then implants in the uterine wall.

Q: After ovulation, the ruptured follicle becomes the ___, which secretes progesterone.
A. Primary follicle
B. Dominant follicle
C. Zona pellucida
*D. Corpus luteum
Explain: The LH-driven corpus luteum forms from the ruptured follicle and secretes progesterone to maintain the endometrium.

Q: During the uterine (menstrual) cycle, estrogen is chiefly responsible for:
*A. Rebuilding the functional layer of the endometrium after menstruation
B. Triggering menstruation
C. Preventing ovulation
D. Breaking down the corpus luteum
Explain: Rising estrogen in the proliferative phase rebuilds the endometrial functionalis shed during menstruation.

Q: During fetal development, the gonads first form in the:
A. Scrotum
B. Pelvic floor
*C. Abdominal cavity, then descend
D. Inguinal lymph nodes
Explain: Both ovaries and testes develop high in the abdomen and descend; the testes continue into the scrotum.

Q: Development of male external genitalia depends on which hormone?
*A. Dihydrotestosterone (DHT)
B. Estrogen
C. hCG
D. Inhibin
Explain: DHT drives formation of the male external genitalia from the undifferentiated homologous structures.

Q: The normally acidic vaginal environment is transiently neutralized by:
A. Acidic urine
B. Cervical mucus
C. Menstrual blood
*D. Alkaline semen
Explain: Semen is alkaline, briefly raising vaginal pH so sperm can survive the otherwise acidic environment.

Q: Which structure is part of the vulva (external female genitalia)?
A. Ovary
B. Uterus
C. Fallopian tube
*D. Labia majora
Explain: The vulva includes the mons pubis, labia majora and minora, clitoris, and vestibule; the ovary, uterus, and tubes are internal.

Q: Milk is produced in which structures of the mammary gland?
A. The nipple
*B. The alveoli of the lobules
C. The surrounding adipose tissue
D. The lactiferous sinus
Explain: Secretory alveoli within the lobules produce milk, which drains through ducts to the nipple during lactation.

Q: Which prenatal assessment can be performed from a sample of the mother's blood?
A. Amniocentesis
*B. Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)
C. Chorionic villus sampling
D. Fetal surgery
Explain: NIPT analyzes fetal DNA circulating in maternal blood; amniocentesis and CVS instead sample amniotic fluid or placental tissue.

Q: According to your notes, the placenta becomes fully established at approximately:
A. Week 1-2
B. Week 4-6
*C. Week 10-12
D. Week 20
Explain: The placenta begins developing early but is not fully functional until roughly weeks 10-12, when it takes over hormone production.

Q: The major organs first appear during development at approximately:
A. Weeks 1 to 2
*B. Weeks 4 to 8
C. Weeks 20 to 24
D. Weeks 36 to 40
Explain: Most major organ systems begin forming during weeks 4-8 (the embryonic period), a time of high sensitivity to teratogens.

Q: In vitro fertilization (IVF) involves:
A. Implanting a donor uterus
B. Blocking the fallopian tubes
C. Stimulating natural ovulation only
*D. Combining sperm and egg outside the body
Explain: IVF fertilizes the egg with sperm in the lab, then transfers the resulting embryo into the uterus.
