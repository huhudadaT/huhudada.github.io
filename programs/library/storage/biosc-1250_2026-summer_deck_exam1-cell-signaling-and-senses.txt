Q: What are the three methods of cell-to-cell communication?
A: Gap junctions, cell-to-cell binding, and extracellular chemical messengers.

Q: What membrane proteins form gap junctions?
A: Connexins.

Q: What are the tunnels formed by connexins called?
A: Connexons.

Q: What is the function of gap junctions?
A: Allow rapid diffusion and communication between neighboring cells.

Q: What are the three main types of extracellular chemical messengers?
A: Hormones, neurotransmitters, and local mediators.

Q: What are hormones?
A: Chemical messengers carried by the blood to distant target cells.

Q: What are neurotransmitters?
A: Chemical messengers released from a neuron into a synapse to reach a nearby target cell.

Q: What are local mediators?
A: Extracellular messengers that act locally without entering the bloodstream.

Q: What is autocrine signaling?
A: A cell secretes a messenger that acts on itself.

Q: What is paracrine signaling?
A: A cell secretes a messenger that acts on nearby cells.

Q: Which local mediators are important in cellular growth and development?
A: Cytokines.

Q: Which local mediator causes blood vessel dilation?
A: Nitric oxide.

Q: What are eicosanoids made from?
A: Arachidonic acid.

Q: What two enzymes process arachidonic acid into eicosanoids?
A: Cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase.

Q: What are the three main types of eicosanoids?
A: Prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and thromboxanes.

Q: What do leukotrienes do?
A: Stimulate chemotaxis of leukocytes and mediate inflammation.

Q: What do thromboxanes do?
A: Constrict blood vessels and promote platelet activation.

Q: What enzyme do NSAIDs block?
A: Cyclooxygenase.

Q: What enzyme do glucocorticoids inhibit?
A: Phospholipase A2.

Q: What enzyme does aspirin inhibit?
A: Cyclooxygenase.

Q: How many hydrocarbon rings do steroids contain?
A: Four.

Q: What are the four properties of messenger-receptor binding?
A: Specificity, affinity, saturation, and competition.

Q: What is affinity?
A: The strength at which a ligand binds to its receptor.

Q: What is receptor saturation?
A: The degree to which receptors are bound by messengers.

Q: What is an agonist?
A: A drug that binds and activates a receptor, mimicking the natural messenger.

Q: What is an antagonist?
A: A substance that binds and blocks a receptor.

Q: An antagonist has affinity but lacks what?
A: Efficacy.

Q: What is receptor down-regulation?
A: A decrease in receptor number when a messenger is in excess.

Q: What is receptor up-regulation?
A: An increase in receptor number when a messenger is deficient.

Q: What is signal transduction?
A: The process by which a signal molecule is converted into a cellular response.

Q: What is a first messenger?
A: The extracellular messenger that binds the receptor.

Q: What is a second messenger?
A: The intracellular messenger that relays the signal.

Q: What does a protein kinase do?
A: Adds a phosphate group to a target protein.

Q: Where are steroid hormone receptors located?
A: Inside the cell, in the cytosol or nucleus.

Q: What is the second messenger in the nitric oxide pathway?
A: cGMP.

Q: What activates a receptor tyrosine kinase?
A: Receptor dimerization.

Q: Janus kinase is an example of what receptor type?
A: Enzyme-coupled receptor.

Q: What does STAT stand for?
A: Signal transducer and activator of transcription.

Q: What enzyme converts ATP to cAMP?
A: Adenylyl cyclase.

Q: What enzyme breaks down cAMP?
A: Phosphodiesterase.

Q: What enzyme produces IP3 and DAG?
A: Phospholipase C.

Q: What molecule does phospholipase C break down to make IP3 and DAG?
A: PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol bisphosphate).

Q: What does IP3 do?
A: Releases Ca2+ from intracellular stores.

Q: What three second messengers work together in the phospholipase C pathway?
A: IP3, DAG, and Ca2+.

Q: What G protein transduces smell signals from odorants?
A: Golf protein.

Q: What bacterium produces cholera toxin?
A: Vibrio cholerae.

Q: What G protein does cholera toxin permanently activate?
A: Gs.

Q: What G protein does pertussis toxin permanently inactivate?
A: Gi.

Q: What is signal amplification?
A: A cascade that lets a messenger produce large effects at low concentrations.

Q: What two structures make up the central nervous system?
A: The brain and spinal cord.

Q: What do afferent (sensory) neurons do?
A: Carry information to the CNS.

Q: What do efferent (motor) neurons do?
A: Carry commands away from the CNS.

Q: What is the basic functional unit of the nervous system?
A: The neuron.

Q: What are the three main parts of a neuron?
A: Cell body, dendrites, and axon.

Q: What two motor proteins drive axonal transport?
A: Kinesin and dynein.

Q: What type of neuron makes up about 99% of neurons in the body?
A: Interneurons.

Q: What is the most numerous type of neuroglia?
A: Astrocytes.

Q: Which glia help maintain the blood-brain barrier?
A: Astrocytes.

Q: Which CNS glia form the myelin sheath?
A: Oligodendrocytes.

Q: Which glia are the phagocytes of the CNS?
A: Microglia.

Q: Which glia produce and circulate cerebrospinal fluid?
A: Ependymal cells.

Q: Which glia form the myelin sheath in the PNS?
A: Schwann cells.

Q: What is another name for Schwann cells?
A: Neurolemmocytes.

Q: What is the function of the myelin sheath?
A: Insulates axons and increases the speed of action potential conduction.

Q: What are the gaps in the myelin sheath called?
A: Nodes of Ranvier.

Q: What makes up white matter?
A: Myelinated axons.

Q: What makes up gray matter?
A: Neuron cell bodies.

Q: What is neuroplasticity?
A: The nervous system's ability to change and rewire throughout life.

Q: In which part of the nervous system can axons regenerate?
A: The PNS.

Q: What structure do Schwann cells form to guide axon regrowth?
A: A regeneration tube.

Q: Why can't axons regenerate in the CNS?
A: Astrocytes form scar tissue that blocks axonal repair.

Q: What are the four types of ion channels in neurons?
A: Leak, ligand-gated, mechanically-gated, and voltage-gated.

Q: What opens a ligand-gated ion channel?
A: A specific chemical (ligand) stimulus.

Q: What opens a mechanically-gated ion channel?
A: Mechanical stimulation such as touch, pressure, or stretch.

Q: What opens a voltage-gated ion channel?
A: A change in membrane potential.

Q: Where are voltage-gated channels found?
A: On the axons of neurons.

Q: What is membrane potential?
A: The voltage that exists across the plasma membrane.

Q: What is the resting membrane potential of a neuron?
A: -70 mV.

Q: Which ion has the greatest influence on the resting membrane potential?
A: K+ (potassium).

Q: Why does K+ most influence the resting potential?
A: Neurons at rest are most permeable to K+.

Q: State Ohm's law.
A: I = V/R.

Q: What is an equilibrium potential?
A: The point at which chemical and electrical gradients are equal and opposite, so there is no net ion flow.

Q: What equation calculates the equilibrium potential for a single ion?
A: The Nernst equation.

Q: What is the equilibrium potential for K+?
A: -90 mV.

Q: What is the equilibrium potential for Na+?
A: +60 mV.

Q: What equation calculates membrane potential using multiple ion permeabilities?
A: The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation.

Q: What is a graded potential?
A: A small deviation from the resting membrane potential.

Q: What does depolarization mean?
A: The membrane potential becomes less polarized (more positive).

Q: What does hyperpolarization mean?
A: The membrane potential becomes more polarized (more negative).

Q: What is the typical threshold potential for an action potential?
A: -55 mV.

Q: What is a subthreshold stimulus?
A: A stimulus not strong enough to reach threshold.

Q: What does the all-or-none principle state?
A: Once threshold is reached, the action potential fires fully regardless of stimulus strength.

Q: Where is the trigger zone for the action potential?
A: The axon hillock.

Q: What are the three phases of an action potential?
A: Depolarizing, repolarizing, and after-hyperpolarizing.

Q: To what value does the membrane rise during the depolarizing phase?
A: About +30 mV.

Q: What happens during the after-hyperpolarizing phase?
A: The membrane potential undershoots to about -90 mV.

Q: What is a synapse?
A: The point where a neuron communicates with another neuron or target tissue.

Q: What ion triggers neurotransmitter release at a synapse?
A: Ca2+.

Q: What is the Ca2+ sensor for neurotransmitter release?
A: Synaptotagmin.

Q: Which synapse type is faster, electrical or chemical?
A: Electrical.

Q: What connects cells at an electrical synapse?
A: Gap junctions.

Q: What is an EPSP?
A: An excitatory postsynaptic potential, a depolarization of the postsynaptic membrane.

Q: What is an IPSP?
A: An inhibitory postsynaptic potential, a hyperpolarization of the postsynaptic membrane.

Q: What are ionotropic receptors?
A: Receptors that function as ligand-gated ion channels.

Q: What are metabotropic receptors?
A: Receptors that function through a G-protein.

Q: What type of receptor is the nicotinic ACh receptor?
A: Ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channel).

Q: What type of receptor is the muscarinic ACh receptor?
A: Metabotropic (G-protein coupled).

Q: What enzyme degrades acetylcholine?
A: Acetylcholinesterase.

Q: What is temporal summation?
A: Summation of graded potentials arriving in rapid succession from one source.

Q: What is spatial summation?
A: Summation of graded potentials arriving at the same time from different sources.

Q: What amino acid are catecholamines synthesized from?
A: Tyrosine.

Q: What neurotransmitter is GABA derived from?
A: Glutamate.

Q: Which neurotransmitter is typically excitatory?
A: Glutamate.

Q: Which two neurotransmitters are typically inhibitory?
A: GABA and glycine.

Q: What are the four types of neural circuits?
A: Diverging, converging, reverberating, and parallel after-discharge.

Q: What is a diverging circuit?
A: One presynaptic neuron synapses with several postsynaptic neurons.

Q: What is a converging circuit?
A: One postsynaptic neuron receives input from several sources.

Q: What is a reverberating circuit?
A: Later neurons feed back to stimulate earlier ones, repeating the signal.

Q: What are the three connective tissue membranes protecting the brain?
A: The meninges.

Q: Name the three meninges from outer to inner.
A: Dura mater, arachnoid mater, pia mater.

Q: Which meninx is the toughest and outermost?
A: Dura mater.

Q: Which meninx is cobweb-like and in the middle?
A: Arachnoid mater.

Q: Which meninx adheres directly to the brain surface?
A: Pia mater.

Q: What is the blood-brain barrier?
A: A selective barrier of specialized capillaries controlling what enters the brain.

Q: What two structural components form the blood-brain barrier?
A: Tight junctions and astrocytes.

Q: How does glucose cross the blood-brain barrier?
A: By facilitated transport.

Q: What are circumventricular organs?
A: Brain regions that lack a blood-brain barrier and monitor blood chemistry.

Q: What produces cerebrospinal fluid?
A: The choroid plexus.

Q: What cells within the choroid plexus produce CSF?
A: Tufts of ependymal cells.

Q: How many ventricles does the brain have?
A: Four.

Q: What are the two protective functions of CSF?
A: Mechanical protection and chemical protection.

Q: What condition is caused by excess CSF on the brain?
A: Hydrocephalus.

Q: How many pairs of cranial nerves are there?
A: Twelve.

Q: What is cranial nerve I?
A: Olfactory.

Q: What is cranial nerve II?
A: Optic.

Q: What is cranial nerve III?
A: Oculomotor.

Q: What is cranial nerve IV?
A: Trochlear.

Q: What is cranial nerve V?
A: Trigeminal.

Q: What is cranial nerve VI?
A: Abducens.

Q: What is cranial nerve VII?
A: Facial.

Q: What is cranial nerve VIII?
A: Vestibulocochlear.

Q: What is cranial nerve IX?
A: Glossopharyngeal.

Q: What is cranial nerve X?
A: Vagus.

Q: What is cranial nerve XI?
A: Accessory.

Q: What is cranial nerve XII?
A: Hypoglossal.

Q: Which cranial nerve controls tongue movement?
A: Hypoglossal (XII).

Q: Which cranial nerve is responsible for balance?
A: Vestibulocochlear (VIII).

Q: Which cranial nerve provides parasympathetic control of the viscera?
A: Vagus (X).

Q: What are the three parts of the brain stem?
A: Midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata.

Q: What are the three parts of the diencephalon?
A: Thalamus, hypothalamus, and pineal gland.

Q: What midbrain nucleus produces dopamine and is linked to Parkinson's?
A: Substantia nigra.

Q: What are the four colliculi collectively called?
A: Corpora quadrigemina.

Q: What is the function of the superior colliculi?
A: Vision and tracking movement.

Q: What is the function of the inferior colliculi?
A: Auditory startle reflexes.

Q: What two vital centers are located in the medulla oblongata?
A: Cardiovascular center and respiratory center.

Q: What does the cerebellum regulate?
A: Posture, balance, and coordination of movement.

Q: What is the main function of the thalamus?
A: Relays most sensory input to the cerebral cortex.

Q: Which sensory input does NOT pass through the thalamus?
A: Olfactory (smell).

Q: What gland secretes melatonin?
A: The pineal gland.

Q: What does melatonin control?
A: Sleep/wake cycles.

Q: What is the body's internal clock nucleus in the hypothalamus?
A: The suprachiasmatic nucleus.

Q: What is the largest part of the brain?
A: The cerebrum.

Q: How thick is the cerebral cortex?
A: 2-4 mm.

Q: How many layers does the cerebral cortex have?
A: Six.

Q: What are the main input cells of the cerebral cortex?
A: Stellate (granule) cells.

Q: What are the main output cells of the cerebral cortex?
A: Pyramidal cells.

Q: What are the ridges of the cerebrum called?
A: Gyri.

Q: What are the grooves of the cerebrum called?
A: Sulci.

Q: What dural fold separates the two cerebral hemispheres?
A: Falx cerebri.

Q: What dural fold separates the cerebellum from the cerebrum?
A: Tentorium cerebelli.

Q: Where is the primary somatosensory cortex located?
A: Postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe.

Q: Where is the primary visual cortex located?
A: The occipital lobe.

Q: Where is the primary auditory cortex located?
A: The temporal lobe.

Q: Where is the gustatory cortex located?
A: The insula.

Q: Where is the primary motor cortex located?
A: Precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe.

Q: What does contralateral control mean?
A: Each side of the brain controls the opposite side of the body.

Q: What is Broca's area responsible for?
A: The articulation (production) of speech.

Q: What is Wernicke's area responsible for?
A: Interpreting the meaning of words.

Q: What does the prefrontal cortex control?
A: Personality, intellect, and complex learning.

Q: What are the three basal nuclei?
A: Globus pallidus, putamen, and caudate nucleus.

Q: What brain system is involved in emotions?
A: The limbic system.

Q: What system maintains wakefulness?
A: The reticular activating system.

Q: What is long-term potentiation?
A: A long-lasting strengthening of synapses underlying learning and memory.

Q: What two glutamate receptors mediate long-term potentiation?
A: AMPA and NMDA receptors.

Q: What are the four steps in the process of sensation?
A: Stimulation, transduction, generation of action potentials, and integration.

Q: What type of potential is generated when a sensory receptor is stimulated?
A: A receptor (graded) potential.

Q: What do mechanoreceptors detect?
A: Mechanical stimuli like deformation, stretch, or bending.

Q: What do thermoreceptors detect?
A: Changes in temperature.

Q: What do photoreceptors detect?
A: Light striking the retina.

Q: What do chemoreceptors detect?
A: Chemicals (taste, smell, and body fluids).

Q: What do nociceptors detect?
A: Painful stimuli from tissue damage.

Q: What is a receptive field?
A: The physical area that, when stimulated, activates a sensory neuron.

Q: What are the four attributes of a stimulus that get encoded?
A: Modality, location, intensity, and duration.

Q: What is stimulus modality?
A: The unique type of sensation, such as pain, touch, vision, taste, or hearing.

Q: What are labeled lines?
A: Neural pathways that carry a specific modality to a specific cortical region.

Q: How is stimulus intensity encoded?
A: By action potential frequency and the number of receptors activated.

Q: How is stimulus duration encoded?
A: By the duration of action potentials in the sensory neuron.

Q: What is lateral inhibition?
A: Inhibition of receptors at a stimulus border relative to those at the center.

Q: What is two-point discrimination?
A: The ability to perceive two separate points of touch.

Q: What do slowly adapting receptors do?
A: Respond continuously as long as the stimulus persists.

Q: What do rapidly adapting receptors do?
A: Respond at stimulus onset and removal, then stop.

Q: Which spinal tract carries touch, pressure, vibration, and proprioception?
A: The dorsal columns.

Q: Which spinal tract carries pain, itch, temperature, and tickle?
A: The spinothalamic tract.

Q: What does it mean for a sensory pathway to decussate?
A: It crosses to the opposite side of the body.

Q: Which corpuscle is a rapidly adapting receptor for flutter and stroking?
A: Meissner's corpuscle.

Q: Which corpuscle is a slowly adapting receptor for steady pressure and texture?
A: Merkel's corpuscle.

Q: Which corpuscle detects vibration and deep pressure?
A: Pacinian (lamellated) corpuscle.

Q: Which corpuscle is a slowly adapting receptor for skin stretch?
A: Ruffini corpuscle.

Q: What is another name for the Pacinian corpuscle?
A: Lamellated corpuscle.

Q: What are the three types of nociceptors?
A: Mechanical, thermal, and polymodal.

Q: What fibers carry fast pain?
A: Myelinated A-delta fibers.

Q: What fibers carry slow pain?
A: Unmyelinated C fibers.

Q: What channels transduce temperature and painful stimuli?
A: TRP (transient receptor potential) channels.

Q: What is referred pain?
A: Pain from an internal organ felt at a different surface location.

Q: What does the gate control theory of pain state?
A: A mechanical stimulus like touch or pressure can suppress pain.

Q: What are the three endogenous opioids?
A: Enkephalins, endorphins, and dynorphins.

Q: What neuropeptide do endogenous opioids block the release of?
A: Substance P.

Q: What are the two main neurotransmitters of first-order pain neurons?
A: Glutamate and substance P.

Q: What are the three types of proprioceptors?
A: Golgi tendon organs, muscle spindles, and joint kinesthetic receptors.

Q: What is kinesthesia?
A: The perception of body movement.

Q: What do muscle spindles detect?
A: Muscle length (stretch).

Q: What cells in the olfactory epithelium are stem cells?
A: Basal cells.

Q: How long do olfactory receptor cells live?
A: About two months.

Q: Which sensation reaches the cortex without synapsing in the thalamus?
A: Smell (olfaction).

Q: Where do olfactory tract axons project to produce emotional responses to odors?
A: The limbic system.

Q: What is the loss of smell called?
A: Anosmia.

Q: What are chemicals that stimulate taste receptors called?
A: Tastants.

Q: What are the five primary tastes?
A: Sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami.

Q: What are the three cell types in a taste bud?
A: Supporting cells, gustatory receptor cells, and basal cells.

Q: What opening lets tastants reach gustatory receptor cells?
A: The taste pore.

Q: Which tongue papillae lack taste buds in humans?
A: Filiform papillae.

Q: What G protein is involved in taste transduction?
A: Gustducin.

Q: What are the two types of photoreceptors?
A: Rods and cones.

Q: What are rods responsible for?
A: High-sensitivity vision in dim light.

Q: What are cones responsible for?
A: Color vision.

Q: What are the three types of cones?
A: Blue, green, and red.

Q: What photopigment is found in rods?
A: Rhodopsin.

Q: What vitamin deficiency causes night blindness?
A: Vitamin A.

Q: What test screens for red-green color blindness?
A: The Ishihara test.

Q: What part of the retina has no rods and the sharpest vision?
A: The fovea centralis.

Q: What is accommodation?
A: An increase in the curvature of the lens to focus on near objects.

Q: What is the correct sequence of visual signal flow in the retina?
A: Photoreceptors to bipolar cells to ganglion cells.

Q: What cells form the optic nerve?
A: Ganglion cells.

Q: What is the function of the optic chiasm?
A: Allows binocular vision and depth perception by crossing fibers.

Q: Which ear hair cells are responsible for sound transduction?
A: Inner hair cells.

Q: What do outer hair cells do?
A: Enhance the sensitivity of inner hair cells.

Q: What determines the pitch of a sound?
A: Its frequency.

Q: In what units is sound intensity measured?
A: Decibels.

Q: What membrane discriminates pitch in the cochlea?
A: The basilar membrane.

Q: Along which nerve does auditory information travel to the brain?
A: The vestibulocochlear (VIII) nerve.

Q: What are the two otolithic organs?
A: The utricle and saccule.

Q: What do the utricle and saccule detect?
A: Linear acceleration and head tilt.

Q: What do the semicircular ducts detect?
A: Rotational acceleration.

Q: What are the calcium carbonate crystals in the otolithic organs called?
A: Otoliths.

Q: What disease results from excess endolymph in the membranous labyrinth?
A: Ménière's disease.

Q: What maneuver repositions dislodged otoliths to treat vertigo?
A: The Epley maneuver.
